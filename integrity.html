The Parable No One Wants to Preach

To kick off the first essay in this series, I'd like to begin with one of the most misunderstood parables delivered by Jesus.

"There was a rich man whose manager was accused of wasting his possessions. So he called him in and asked him, 'What is this I hear about you? Give an account of your management, because you cannot be a manager any longer.'

"The manager said to himself, 'What shall I do now? My master is taking away my job. I'm not strong enough to dig, and I'm ashamed to beg—I know what I'll do so that, when I lose my job here, people will welcome me into their houses.'

"So he called in each one of his master's debtors. He asked the first, 'How much do you owe my master?'

"'Nine hundred gallons of olive oil,' he replied.

"The manager told him, 'Take your bill, sit down quickly, and make it four hundred and fifty.'

"Then he asked the second, 'And how much do you owe?'

"'A thousand bushels of wheat,' he replied.

"He told him, 'Take your bill and make it eight hundred.'

"The master commended the dishonest manager because he had acted shrewdly. For the people of this world are more shrewd in dealing with their own kind than are the people of the light. I tell you, use worldly wealth to gain friends for yourselves, so that when it is gone, you will be welcomed into eternal dwellings.

"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much. So if you have not been trustworthy in handling worldly wealth, who will trust you with true riches? And if you have not been trustworthy with someone else's property, who will give you property of your own?

"No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money."

The Pharisees, who loved money, heard all this and were sneering at Jesus. He said to them, "You are the ones who justify yourselves in the eyes of others, but God knows your hearts. What people value highly is detestable in God's sight."

— Luke 16:1–15

What Jesus Is Really Saying

Did you notice anything strange about this passage? And no, Jesus is not endorsing fraud or cooking the books. However, he is using this situation to give us a hint about how we should be living our lives as Christians. Not with dishonesty, but with shrewdness.

Another way of saying this is: What is going to truly matter when this life is over? Then once you know, invest in those things—relationships, generosity, and things beyond this life.

Worldly wealth is a testing ground. It's not true treasure; it's temporary and ultimately insignificant in eternity. Jesus makes it clear: if we are wasteful with earthly resources, why would God trust us with heavenly ones?

OK, makes sense. Be more altruistic, care for others, spend time with my neighbors. I can work those things in, no problem. Actually, the teaching is still incomplete. Jesus tags on one more thing: no one can serve two masters. You have to choose. Try to hold both and you won't just fail strategically—you'll be divided against yourself and gain nothing.

What Is Integrity, Really?

Let's explore that by talking about integrity.

You probably know one definition: being honest, having strong moral principles. That's true. But I want you to see another layer. The Latin root—integritas—means wholeness, coherence, total unity. Not just "doing the right thing" but being undivided.

Here's how I'd put it: integrity is the virtue of self-construction. You cannot become the person you aspire to be unless everything you do works together—designed, ordered, unified toward the same end.

"If you have integrity, nothing else matters. If you don't have integrity, nothing else matters."

If that sounds abstract, let's make it concrete. What does your bedroom look like right now?

Let's try an exercise. What does your bedroom tell you about purpose? Have you ever walked into a room where there are piles of clothes, or junk is pushed in the corner, or you have to walk around obstacles?

From Piles to Purpose

So what do we do with the piles?

There's a clinical psychologist who's become famous for telling people to clean their room. When pressed on whether that's really all he's asking, he says no—that's just where you start. And it's harder than you think.

Here's why: to spend ten minutes making your room better, you first have to answer a deeper question. What is this room for?

Is your bedroom for sleeping? Then making it better means making it more suitable for rest. Is your living room for conversation? Then the chairs face each other, not the TV. The answer shapes everything.

But most of us don't decide. We're not sure what the room is for—so there are piles. Or we can't decide—so there are piles. Or we say it has multiple purposes—and there are piles.

To answer the question what is this room for requires integrity. It requires turning a value into a decision, translating a desire into action.

Now extend that.

What is your life for?

The Freedom of Deciding

Jesus says no one can serve two masters. Yes, we can have multiple interests—that's part of being human. But what is the overriding, unifying purpose? What's the one thing everything else orbits around?

If you try to hold two ultimate purposes, you won't just fail strategically. You'll end up hating one of them. And you'll resent the God who wanted more for you.

But imagine the freedom of actually deciding.

The freedom of always being where your feet are. Of going to bed knowing your heart and your actions were aligned. Of life not being piles—but a purpose.

That's integrity. Not just honesty. Wholeness.
